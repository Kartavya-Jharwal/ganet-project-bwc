Executive summary

Adaptive Efficiency names this site: a personal frame for the run, borrowing Andrew Lo’s adaptive-markets idea that desks and regimes adjust over time. It is not a claim that we beat the simulation.

Ten-week paper desk: smaller trough drawdown than SPY at the Apr 2 low, slower April re-risk after the ceasefire. Filed memo is the narrative authority for grades. This page is my scan path.

  • Act I (Feb): Foundation book deployed: beta target ~1.0, sector sleeves, diary discipline from day one.
  • Act II (Mar): Section 5 charter override (portfolio last in class with twelve days left), then emergency triage, hedges, and lower exposure. Desk trough -6.44% vs SPY -11.72% on Apr 2.
  • Act III (Apr): Ceasefire window: SPY snapped back. We rebuilt equity exposure too carefully and gave back relative performance.

Faculty sign-off already happened (422 / 430). This microsite is an independent, unofficial archive (not Hult, the professor, or teammates). What follows is my personal take and presentation on the same filed data. If this page disagrees with the Excel close or memo, the filed artifacts win.

    Story

    What happened on the desk

    One crash window, one recovery we chased too slowly: four phases on a stressed Q1 book.

    Architecture vs P&L

    Filed memo line: the risk architecture was defensible, but converting macro views into realized P&L lagged at regime shifts, hedge roll-off, and post-shock re-risk.

    March trough

    Low-beta book plus crisis hedge: peak desk drawdown about -6.5% vs SPY -8.5% in late March. Not headline alpha, but relative protection when beta was near zero.

    April re-risk

    After the 8 Apr ceasefire, slow re-entry into equities meant we under-captured the snap-back. Discipline on the way down, hesitation when speed mattered on the way up.

    Honest frame

    Above-average regime awareness, below-average execution precision in fast windows. The log shows losers held roughly twice as long as winners.

    Charter vs close

    We signed up for moderate risk, a SPY benchmark, and a filed memo target of ~10% p.a. with ~1.0 average beta (Foundation ~1.0, Growth ~1.1, Crisis ~0.0, Ceasefire ~0.6). We kept logs and limits, but the closing return was red on both SPY and our own stated goal.

    Phase 3 was not a calm risk dial-down: with twelve trading days left and the book last in class, we invoked Section 5 of the charter. The narrower win at the Apr 2 trough: desk -6.44% vs SPY -11.72% (+5.28pp).

    Research

    How we researched, logged, and stress-tested decisions

    The brief asked for process evidence, not headline chasing. Eight operators split execution, IPS compliance, and journal discipline, with a Python overlay as a confidence check in the diary, not a trade signal.

    01

    Operating model

    Three parallel lanes on one book. The brief did not ask us to invent the mandate. It asked us to run inside it and prove we did.

    Execution lane

    StockTrak fills, sizing, stops, and crisis-overlay trades. One operator carried primary execution while the desk debated macro in parallel.

    IPS / compliance lane

    Position limits, beta targets, and mandate checks against the filed IPS. A sub-team tracked drift before trades cleared committee chat.

    Journal lane

    Rationale for every open and close, win or loss, plus bi-weekly logs translating diary entries into trade-level justification.

    02

    Desk research lanes

    What the deliverables list required, expressed through sleeves, screens, and memo sections, not a single quant model.

    Macro / top-down

    War headlines, rates, and ceasefire windows drove hedge and re-risk timing.

    Sector sleeves

    AI, defence, financials, and defensive retail expressed as ETF and single-name sleeves.

    Technical timing

    Stops and trailing stops on extended names, with trend confirmation before adds.

    Fundamentals / 10-K

    Quality and balance-sheet screens on high-conviction singles alongside ETF beta.

    ESG review

    Committee-facing lens on contested names: narrative risk, not a separate book.

    Quant / AI pitches

    Screens and pitches informed debate. Execution still ran through StockTrak and IPS.

    03

    Confidence layer

    Beyond the brief: a Python overlay on the closed trading universe. It answered “did we break our own rules?” after volatile weeks. Cited in diaries, never wired to live execution.

    • Confidence, not alpha: a structured check on diary claims when macro moved faster than committee chat.
    • Closed universe: in quant-tooling terms, we ran closed-universe checks on names we actually held. The model did not suggest new tickers or resizings outside that set.
    • Policy mirror: flag beta and sector drift vs IPS before the next defense meeting.

    Principles

    What we would repeat

    Four lines we would defend in a PM review or faculty debrief.

    Honest marks

    Report the loss first. Trough relative win vs SPY is context, not a victory lap.

    Faculty and future employers should see the red number before the hedge story: that is how we would present to an IC today.

    Process under fire

    Logs, diary, and filed limits (e.g. 25% max single name) mattered more than one lucky trade.

    The 147/150 architecture score reflects reproducible records, not P&L. The discipline we took into compliance and risk internships.

    Inverse hedge lost money outright

    PSQ/SH cost -$19,205 on the Apr 2 tariff rally. AA/CENX (+$17,169) and lower exposure at the trough did more protecting than inverse ETFs.

    SH/PSQ behave like short futures, not convex puts. Next desk would size 1–2% NAV in OTM SPY puts instead, and still pre-write a ceasefire re-risk playbook.

    Quant as mirror

    Python checked our story. It did not trade the book.

    It exists so we could ask “did we break our own rules?” after each week.

      The brief

      Manage $1,000,000 in simulated capital as if it were real. Operate inside constraints set in advance, not negotiated after the fact.

      Report performance the way a professional fund reports to investors: in risk-adjusted terms, not raw returns.

      The operating environment

      StockTrak mirrors a live brokerage: real-time pricing, executed orders, margin, and shorts, settled in paper capital, not real currency.

      Each team's account functioned as a mock investment book: positions, cash, and realized / unrealized P&L tracked like an institutional ledger. The mechanics were real. The capital was not.

      The Investment Policy Statement (risk tolerance, eligible instruments, operating limits) was issued by the instructor before trading opened. The task was to operate inside a mandate already set, and justify every decision within it.

      Who the brief was written for

      Deliberately open-ended. No prior finance background assumed. Legible to someone who reads a stock chart as green-up / red-down, yet rigorous enough to grade on Sharpe and risk-adjusted return by semester end.

      The constraints

      • $1,000,000 simulated starting capital
      • US equities and ETFs in practice. Treasuries, margin, and shorts permitted under the IPS
      • No single security above 25% of portfolio value
      • $10 commission per trade. 300-trade semester ceiling
      • 3% annualized interest on uninvested cash
      • Eight teams, identical rules. Five-person team cap removed this run

      The deliverables

      • Minimum four trades per week for the full semester
      • Trading diary: rationale for every open and close, win or loss
      • Bi-weekly logs translating diary into trade-level justification
      • Analysis spanning technicals, quant screens, AI pitches, top-down / bottom-up work, ESG, 10-K review, and forward book construction
      • Final report and client presentation in plain language, not finance jargon

      Team BWC filed targets

      The committee memo stated moderate risk, benchmarked to SPY, with an expected return of ~10% per annum and an average portfolio beta target of ~1.0. Phase net-beta targets in the marks book: Foundation ~1.0, Growth ~1.1, Crisis ~0.0, Ceasefire ~0.6. Term close was -4.37% total vs SPY -2.29%.

      Why it was structured this way

      The client was not buying speculation or headline-chasing. They wanted evidence that disciplined process, applied consistently and explained clearly, could grow capital while managing risk. Grading weighted process and logs heavily. Headline return still missed.

      Desk-level post-mortem

      Seven closed round-trips in the Excel export. Only two were winners. Losses were concentrated: a handful of stop-outs and crisis-phase names (semis, inverse hedges during the Apr 2 tariff rally) drove most of the negative expectancy, not a long tail of tiny scratches.

      • Structural (would repeat): Terminal beta 0.63 after a Crisis swing from ~1.0 target. AA/CENX metals (+$17,169) buffered trough downside vs SPY. Inverse ETFs were a net drag, not protection.
      • Charter override: With twelve days left and the book last in class, we invoked Section 5: emergency triage, not a calm risk dial-down.
      • Incidental (bad luck): PSQ/SH reversed on the Apr 2 tariff rally (Inverse ETF Reversal sub-event, -1.34%): policy surprise on a short-futures sleeve, not a slow unwind.
      • Execution (would fix): After the 8 Apr ceasefire, SPY was nearly flat to start while we were still -4.8%. We added equity slowly and finished -4.37% vs SPY -2.29%.

      Commissions and dividends were immaterial ($680 fees, $2,044 dividends): sizing and timing book, not an income book.

      We trailed SPY by about 208 basis points on the term and missed our filed ~10% p.a. target. Trough outperformance vs SPY came from metals and lower exposure, not inverse hedges. We re-risked too slowly after the bounce.

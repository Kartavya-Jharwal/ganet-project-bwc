Academic investment challenges are experiential, real-world competitions that let students simulate portfolio management, valuation, and financial analysis without risking actual capital. Top business schools use them to bridge the gap between financial theory and practice.

These competitions—often judged by practitioners—require teams to operate under explicit constraints, produce institutional-quality artifacts, and defend their strategy in writing and orally.

    Desk-level post-mortem

    Seven closed round-trips in the Excel export; only two were winners. Losses were concentrated: a handful of stop-outs and crisis-phase names (semis, inverse hedges during the Apr 2 tariff rally) drove most of the negative expectancy, not a long tail of tiny scratches.

    • Structural (would repeat): Low-beta construction and crisis hedges in March preserved capital vs SPY when the selloff was violent.
    • Incidental (bad luck): Inverse ETF hedges lost when policy headlines ripped risk-on on 2 Apr: right idea, wrong day for the instrument.
    • Execution (would fix): After the 8 Apr ceasefire, SPY was nearly flat to start while we were still -4.8%; we added equity slowly and finished -4.37% vs SPY -2.29%.

    Commissions and dividends were immaterial to the story ($680 fees, $2,044 dividends): this was a sizing and timing book, not an income book.

    What we would repeat

    Report the loss first. March relative win is context, not a victory lap.

    Logs, IPS (our written risk limits), and diary mattered more than one lucky trade.

    Tech

    Quant rigor layer

    We built a separate Python validator as a pro-grade application: a reproducible way to sanity-check risk, attribution, and “are we telling the truth?” against the desk’s own logs.

    Non-technical TL;DR

    This layer generates confidence signals, not trade signals. It is an audit overlay built from the same closed universe the desk actually traded, plus forward-cast stress tests (Monte Carlo). It exists to separate story from data — and to avoid mixing the overlay with the graded Excel marks.

    Engineering docs GitHub repo full-metrics.json Full engineering map

    Why we built a quant layer

    • Confidence, not alpha: we wanted an objective “how sure are we?” meter so diary claims weren’t vibes.
    • Closed universe realism: the validator uses the tickers we actually held/traded, so it audits behavior rather than inventing a new research universe.
    • Backtest + forward-cast: it replays what happened based on trades, then stress-tests what could happen next (Monte Carlo paths).
    • Policy discipline: catch accidental beta/sector drift vs our IPS and committee intent.

    What the core model did (data → math → signals)

    We treated the quant layer like a small product: ingest many sources, normalize them, compute risk/structure metrics, then publish a compact confidence signal into our operator surfaces (CLI dashboards and tooling). No Streamlit app drove decisions.

    Inputs (multi-source ingest)

    • Desk truth: Excel close marks + trading log (the graded source of record)
    • Market prices: vendor pulls + fallbacks (daily bars / history)
    • Macro context: rates / macro series for regime labeling
    • News + filings: feeds used for tagging and post-hoc explanation, not for “live” trading calls

    Data prep (make it computable)

    • Sanitize + de-duplicate + time-align sources
    • Pivot into matrices for ML/statistics (returns, covariances, factor regressions)
    • Cache and persist the “wide” views so the expensive reshapes don’t repeat

    What the quant layer computes

    • GraphicalLassoCV sparse inverse covariance on the return matrix, then MST linkage for stable clusters.
    • Fama-French / Carhart / q-factor OLS on portfolio returns to see accidental factor bets.
    • HRP (hrp_sizer.py) weights from the estimated covariance graph.
    • Black-Litterman + MVO (optimizer.py) policy weights vs desk weights for gap analysis.
    • Brinson-Fachler attribution of allocation vs selection vs the trading log.
    • Cornish-Fisher VaR and jump-augmented Monte Carlo on the overlay path (not Gaussian VaR alone).

    What came out (confidence signals)

    • Confidence score: a compact readout combining structure (correlations / clusters), factor exposure, and tail-risk flags.
    • Operator surfaces: terminal-first (CLI dashboard) and notebook-style analysis. The goal was fast review, not a flashy web UI.
    • Human accountability: scores were referenced in diaries and debriefs so the team could align on “are we guessing?” vs “are we measuring?”

    Weighting pipeline

    Sparse precision via GraphicalLassoCV, MST linkage, then hierarchical risk parity weights.

    A
    GraphicalLassoCV

    Cross-validated L1 precision; denoised covariance for clustering.

    B
    Hierarchical Risk Parity

    Recursive bisection on correlation clusters; risk inversely proportional to cluster variance.

    C
    Minimum spanning tree

    MST dendrogram exposes dependency structure used for HRP linkage.

    Covariance and simulation

    Estimated correlations, tree linkage, risk-parity weights. Forward paths add jump shocks, not a plain bell curve.

    MST Dendrogram

    In plain terms: daily returns follow a drift term, normal volatility, and an extra jump term for gap-down days.

    $$dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t + S_t \, dJ_t$$

    Jump term captures gap risk in a volatile March-April window (not Gaussian tails alone).

    Builders only

    Software map

    Reproducible Python stack in quant_monitor/: ingest → validate → attribute → chart. This is the engineering “map” behind the confidence layer.

    Note

    This is not an alpha research platform. It is a reliability-first build: auditability, repeatable runs, and explainable outputs.

    Why Git and uv

    • Git: an audit trail for changes to assumptions, configs, and outputs — so “why did the score change?” has a concrete diff.
    • uv + lockfile: faster installs + deterministic environments. If the validator reruns on another machine, the results shouldn’t change because a dependency did.

    Short answers we gave in defense

    Macro miss
    We were slow to match the April rally after the ceasefire headline and Brent move.
    Process win
    In late March the desk peak loss was -6.21% vs about -8.54% on SPY. That is the preservation story worth showing an investor.
    Team friction
    Some wanted live alerts from Python. We kept it as a check layer, not a signal feed.

    Wide role split (macro, risk, sectors) vs one shared quantitative standard.
